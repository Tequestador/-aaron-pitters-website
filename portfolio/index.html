01 Creative workflow design
Operational internal workflow · evolving

A production system organized around the story.

An experiment in creative AI became a three-stage architecture: establish the story, secure the references, then produce for the medium. It replaced an earlier idea centered on a single Chief of Staff agent delegating freely to many agent teams.

Where it started

Beginning in 2023, I researched and worked with generative image, video, and language tools. Illustrating Michaelmas revealed a recurring problem: one good image is relatively easy; consistent characters and environments across a narrative are not.

While developing and pitching the television world of Thousand Springs, I began thinking in terms of a film production: distinct responsibilities, dependencies, and coordinated handoffs.

The design decision

My first concept had a central AI agent delegating to specialized groups of agents. Trial and error showed that independent delegation was not enough. The next stage depended on decisions and information established in the previous one.

I separated story analysis, reference consistency, and medium-specific production into defined stages, with structured outputs and human approval points between them.

Guiding constraint: The story must drive the production, rather than allowing whichever generative model is being used to rewrite the story's details.

A decision changed by evidence
The assumption
More specialized agents would mean better intake.

My early preferred approach relied on managed agents and metered API calls. I expected additional orchestration to justify its cost.

The finding
The more economical workflow performed better in this test.

Seven configurations using the same chapter of Michaelmas were compared across 18 extraction and quality measures in April 2026.

41 vs 18

Chronology events found by Run 7 (Opus 4.6 in a Claude Project) versus Run 5 (the same model run as a managed agent).

10 vs 2

Additional canonical story facts preserved by the same two runs.

$0 marginal

Metered API expense on the selected test run, within an existing paid subscription. Not zero total cost.

Interpretation matters: This was a comparison of end-to-end configurations, not a controlled model benchmark. Prompt versions and available project knowledge differed between runs. The result informed a workflow decision under my conditions, not a universal claim about one provider.

What I built and changed

  • Structured story handoffs, separate reference development, and medium-specific production steps.
  • Documented evaluation, routing and cost decisions for different kinds of model work.
  • A supporting Supabase/PostgreSQL data design for story records, approvals, production state and cost tracking.
  • A shift toward subscription-based production and Codex as a central workspace, rather than relying on many separately metered agents.

What it demonstrates

  • Translating real production knowledge into a workable system design.
  • Revising architecture when evaluation contradicts a preferred approach.
  • Balancing capability, creative control, reliability and operating cost.
  • Designing workflows that keep the creator in charge of decisions.
Current scope: An internal pipeline in use and refinement for comics and visual storytelling. Broader media outputs and some deeper MCP-based integrations remain future-facing; they are not claimed as completed productions.

02 Applied AI operations
Public-facing system · tested with fictional leads

AI that prepares the advice. A person who decides.

A consulting inquiry workflow adapted from a familiar business pattern: offer a free, useful first assessment, clarify what the customer really needs, and only recommend paid work when it makes sense.

The business problem

At Appraisal Engine, I saw how free preliminary quotes for vehicle claims could help customers decide whether paying for an appraisal was worthwhile. I brought the same principle to AI consulting: reduce uncertainty and build trust before asking someone to spend money.

I also wanted to offer written, bounded help without site visits, scheduling overhead, or a perpetual support obligation.

The implementation

I designed a four-question web form and a single serverless intake route. It validates the submission, checks bot protection, captures the original inquiry by email, then uses one rubric-guided OpenAI API call to evaluate it.

Its decision brief recommends a free response, a $50 Explainer, a $200 Workflow Review, a decline, or a clarifying question. The brief includes flags, research suggestions, and two draft replies for me to edit.

Deliberately simple: One model call, deterministic code and a human review step. No autonomous agent framework, customer database, or automatic client-facing advice.
01 / FormFour plain-language questions
02 / ValidateServer checks + Turnstile
03 / CaptureOriginal lead emailed first
04 / TriageOpenAI brief + two drafts
05 / ReviewI decide, edit, and reply

Customer receives an ordinary confirmation email. The system never sends model-written recommendations automatically.

A business-rule problem emerged

In an early ten-case evaluation, the model recommended paid service for six inquiries when the answer key expected about three. That violated the spirit of a free first assessment. I revised the rubric to check project size and decision-making authority, then corrected an overcorrection found in later tests.

The goal was not maximizing conversions. It was making recommendations consistent with the service's actual purpose.

Safeguards by design

  • Save the original inquiry before beginning AI processing.
  • Use code to validate the model's output and identify failure conditions.
  • Force the section labeled My take to be blank for my judgment.
  • Send client advice only after manual review and editing.
Evidence boundary: The system was built and tested for my own consulting practice. Tests include documented live submissions with fictional scenarios plus 133 passing offline tests. There is not yet evidence of real customer adoption, revenue, or time saved in live client service. Some regression and privacy/rate-limiting follow-ups remain.
