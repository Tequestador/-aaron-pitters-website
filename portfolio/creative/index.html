01 Image generation
Published · illustrated edition, January 2024

Teaching an image model one consistent look.

In 2023 I began making AI-generated illustrations for my novel Michaelmas. A single good image was easy. Making roughly twenty of them feel like one book was the real problem.

Where it started

In 2023 I started experimenting with AI image tools to create my first illustrated book: a new edition of Michaelmas, set in England in 1727.

I generated the images with DALL·E in ChatGPT, then finished them in Adobe Lightroom and Photoshop.

The problem

Characters and settings had to look the same from one image to the next. Seeding worked well at first. Then the models behind the tool kept changing, and the same approach worked less and less. That is still true today.

In the end I got the images to match through trial and error. What helped most was not a prompt trick but the look I chose: a rustic, antique style that tolerates variation.

What I learned: The visual style you choose can do as much for consistency as any prompt. A look that forgives variation hides it.
Style recipe / six early photographic processes, blended
01
DaguerreotypeSharpness and clarity for characters and central subjects.
02
CalotypeSoft, grainy backgrounds and a dreamlike atmosphere.
03
TintypeDeep shadows and dramatic contrast in the foreground.
04
AmbrotypeTonal depth and luminous highlights.
05
Albumen printFine detail and warm sepia tones.
06
Gum bichromateRestrained color and painterly texture that blends the others.

Rather than applying them one at a time, I combined all six within each illustration, usually at a 16:9 aspect ratio. The result sits between early photography and painting.

I also experimented with period art and architecture: Baroque and Rococo portraiture, Rococo landscapes, Baroque and Palladian architecture, Renaissance painting, and Newtonian optics, with its prismatic light, refraction, and contrasting areas of sharp and soft focus.

Interpretation matters: This is craft, not a controlled experiment. Image tools change often, so treat the recipe as a record of what worked for this book, not a formula that will behave the same way today.

What I did

  • Generated the images with DALL·E in ChatGPT and finished them in Adobe Lightroom and Photoshop.
  • Worked out a blended six-style look through repeated trial and error.
  • Directed the composition and revisions of every image myself.

What it demonstrates

  • Solving visual continuity with art direction as well as prompting.
  • Adapting when the tools change underneath a project.
  • Using AI to extend what I can make as a writer, while the story stays mine.

02 Creative workflow design
Operational internal workflow · evolving

A production system organized around the story.

An experiment in creative AI became a three-stage architecture: establish the story, secure the references, then produce for the medium. It replaced an earlier idea centered on a single Chief of Staff agent delegating freely to many agent teams.

Where it started

Beginning in 2023, I researched and worked with generative image, video, and language tools. Illustrating Michaelmas revealed a recurring problem: one good image is relatively easy; consistent characters and environments across a narrative are not.

While developing and pitching the television world of Thousand Springs, I began thinking in terms of a film production: distinct responsibilities, dependencies, and coordinated handoffs.

The design decision

My first concept had a central AI agent delegating to specialized groups of agents. Trial and error showed that independent delegation was not enough. The next stage depended on decisions and information established in the previous one.

I separated story analysis, reference consistency, and medium-specific production into defined stages, with structured outputs and human approval points between them.

Guiding constraint: The story must drive the production, rather than allowing whichever generative model is being used to rewrite the story's details.
System architecture / staged handoffs
SYSTEM 01
Story intakeExtract characters, locations, events, chronology, and canonical details from source material.
SYSTEM 02
ReferencesDevelop visual guidance and continuity material before final media generation.
SYSTEM 03
ProductionApply story and references to the requirements of a comic page or other medium.

Human review, approval and documented handoffs connect the stages. Creative direction remains with the author.

A decision changed by evidence
The assumption
More specialized agents would mean better intake.

My early preferred approach relied on managed agents and metered API calls. I expected additional orchestration to justify its cost.

The finding
The more economical workflow performed better in this test.

Seven configurations using the same chapter of Michaelmas were compared across 18 extraction and quality measures in April 2026.

41 vs 18

Chronology events found by Run 7 (Opus 4.6 in a Claude Project) versus Run 5 (the same model run as a managed agent).

10 vs 2

Additional canonical story facts preserved by the same two runs.

$0 marginal

Metered API expense on the selected test run, within an existing paid subscription. Not zero total cost.

Interpretation matters: This was a comparison of end-to-end configurations, not a controlled model benchmark. Prompt versions and available project knowledge differed between runs. The result informed a workflow decision under my conditions, not a universal claim about one provider.

What I built and changed

  • Structured story handoffs, separate reference development, and medium-specific production steps.
  • Documented evaluation, routing and cost decisions for different kinds of model work.
  • A supporting Supabase/PostgreSQL data design for story records, approvals, production state and cost tracking.
  • A shift toward subscription-based production and Codex as a central workspace, rather than relying on many separately metered agents.

What it demonstrates

  • Translating real production knowledge into a workable system design.
  • Revising architecture when evaluation contradicts a preferred approach.
  • Balancing capability, creative control, reliability and operating cost.
  • Designing workflows that keep the creator in charge of decisions.
Current scope: An internal pipeline in use and refinement for comics and visual storytelling. Broader media outputs and some deeper MCP-based integrations remain future-facing; they are not claimed as completed productions. Episode one of the House Fly comic is in production and has not been released yet.

03 Creative software
Public app · launched September 2025

A writing app you talk to.

1st Draft is a voice-first storytelling app. You press Start and talk through your story, and your words become text you can shape, instead of sitting at a keyboard to get ideas onto the page.

Where it started

For thirty years I wrote at a computer. I wanted a way to get a story down without being chained to a desk, so I built an app for talking one out. I like to use it on a walk or a hike.

What it does

1st Draft helps writers and non-writers develop, organize, and refine their creative ideas through natural conversation, turning them into structured stories, outlines, and documents.

It began in September 2024 as a GPT-based assistant. I designed, built, tested and publicly launched it as a standalone app in September 2025, managing the requirements, interface design, integration, testing and deployment.

Deliberately simple: Press Start and start talking. One button to begin, and no keyboard required.
01 / StartPress one button
02 / TalkSpeak your story
03 / TranscribeYour words become text
04 / StructureOutlines, stories and documents
05 / RefineKeep developing the idea

Built for writers, and for people who do not think of themselves as writers.

What I built

  • A custom GPT assistant that grew into a standalone app.
  • A voice-first interface: press Start and talk.
  • Transcription of spoken ideas into text, then structure for stories, outlines and documents.

What it demonstrates

  • Designing for the way writers actually work, including away from the desk.
  • Taking an idea from a custom GPT to a public app.
  • Keeping an interface simple enough that anyone can start.
